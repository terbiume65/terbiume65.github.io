Writing samples

Nine pieces of coursework from my BA, kept as I handed them in — only the student number and file metadata are gone. Less a portfolio than a record of how I was taught to argue, and what I did with it.

9 pieces · 110 pages · 2019–2023 · PDF, opens in a new tab

I studied Global Health & Development with a second major in Linguistics, so the range here is not a detour — it is what the degree actually looked like. A policy brief asks you to recommend a decision and cost it; a theory essay asks you to hold one position under pressure; a close reading asks you to notice what a text is doing rather than what it says. Different muscles, same habit of checking the argument for the weak joint.

They are grouped by what the writing has to do, not by year. All are coursework, not publications, and I have left the marks out: the reasoning is the part worth reading.

Policy and persuasion

Writing for a decision-maker: define the problem, weigh the options, cost the proposal, and be honest about what could go wrong.

CAES9922 · 2020 · 12 pages

BEV Exhibition: Green Transport of the Future

A funding proposal for a six-day electric-vehicle exhibition at the HKCEC, covering floor plan, staffing, sponsorship and a budget that holds in both the bullish and the bearish case. The argument is carried by logistics and numbers rather than by citation.

GHAD2001 · 2020 · 16 pages

Controlling global alcohol use

A proposed framework convention on alcohol control, argued the way a WHO brief would: prevalence and burden first, then a menu of interventions scored on effectiveness and political feasibility. It deliberately picks the measures that could win global agreement over the ones that work best in theory.

GHAD3001 · 2021 · 18 pages

Musculoskeletal conditions: a neglected disability epidemic

A mock policy report for a CDC agenda-setting exercise: make the case that musculoskeletal conditions deserve priority, propose direct-access physiotherapy, then map the stakeholders who could stop it. The hard part is arguing for a condition that kills few people but disables many.

Argument and evidence

Essays that answer a question. Each one takes a position early and then spends its words earning it.

GHAD2002 · 2020 · 9 pages

Two questions on global health law

Two exam essays written under time pressure — whether the International Health Regulations can work when the WHO has no enforcement power, and whether the Doha Declaration really protects access to medicines. Both answer “to a moderate extent”, then show the mechanism failing where law meets state interest.

GLBS3101 · 2021 · 5 pages

To what extent does the UN reduce the negative effects of international anarchy?

A short international-relations essay that defines anarchy, then tests the UN against realism, liberalism and constructivism instead of picking one. The verdict — a small extent — follows from noticing that international law is enforced by reputation and retaliation, which powerful states can ignore.

SOWK3099 · 2022 · 13 pages

The impact of zero population growth

Weighs the Malthusian case for zero population growth against the neoclassical case against it, then uses Japan as the test. The conclusion is unsatisfying on purpose: the same demographic fact produces different outcomes depending on labour markets, welfare and expectations.

Language, place, and self

Close reading, and the two pieces where the first person is part of the argument rather than a formality.

CCCH9029 · 2019 · 22 pages

Traditional Chinese medicine and my curriculum

A first-year essay asking where traditional Chinese medicine belongs in a global health degree, answered with a three-stage model pairing TCM with modern medicine in both rich and under-resourced systems. The earliest piece here, and the most personal: the argument is partly about what I wanted to do with the degree.

URBS1003 · 2020 · 3 pages

Reflections on enclave urbanism

A response to He’s paper on enclosed residential forms in Guangzhou, read against a longer history running from agricultural surplus and dynastic segregation to danwei compounds and gated communities. It ends with a suggestion the original did not make: treat “enclaveness” as a scale, not a binary.

LING2003 · 2021 · 12 pages

Sounds, context, and meaning

An argument that semantics cannot be studied as a closed module, built from tone in Mandarin, intonation in French, and the Persian politeness system tarof, where literal meaning misleads an outsider. The examples carry the argument, which is how linguistics usually has to work.
